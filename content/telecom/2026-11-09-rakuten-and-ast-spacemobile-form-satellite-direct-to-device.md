Rakuten and AST SpaceMobile formed a joint venture to provide direct-to-smartphone satellite service in Japan, backed by approximately $1 billion in grants from Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC), according to [Light Reading](https://www.lightreading.com/satellite/rakuten-and-ast-form-d2d-joint-venture-in-japan).

The venture will use AST's Bluebird low-Earth orbit (LEO) satellite constellation to deliver cellular service directly to standard smartphones using midband spectrum. Service launch is scheduled for March 2029. AST currently operates 13 satellites, including one test craft and five first-generation units capable of peak data rates of 100 Mbit/s. The company aims to deploy 45 to 60 satellites to cover key markets including North America, Asia, and Europe. Second-generation Block 2 satellites promise double the throughput of the current generation. Each Bluebird satellite carries phased array antennas measuring approximately 2,400 square feet (223 square meters), orbiting at altitudes between 505 and 590 kilometers or 715 and 740 kilometers.

Rakuten stated the service targets disaster and emergency communications, addressing coverage gaps in Japan's terrain. The timeline aligns with AST's plan to launch its Blacklift rocket, a 20-ton vehicle developed in-house for satellite deployment, with commercial flights expected in late 2028. AST holds launch agreements for over 100 satellites, though limited available space on launch vehicles currently constrains deployment speed. The network architecture differs from broadband-focused LEO systems like Starlink, which uses smaller satellites and requires specialized terminals rather than standard smartphones.

For network operators, the March 2029 service date provides a concrete milestone for satellite direct-to-device integration planning. The Japanese government funding signals official backing for the technology as critical infrastructure, potentially accelerating spectrum coordination and regulatory frameworks for similar deployments. However, the dependency on AST's unproven Blacklift launch vehicle and the need to scale from 13 to 45 operational satellites presents operational risk that practitioners should weigh against terrestrial alternatives.
