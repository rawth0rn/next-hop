LMT Defence, a subsidiary of Latvian operator Latvijas Mobilais Telefons, has developed a mature prototype for a 4G and 5G module intended for military drones [Light Reading](https://www.lightreading.com/5g/latvia-pushes-5g-answer-to-ukraine-s-starlink-problem). The module can operate in either public or private mobile networks using a standard SIM card from any country, allowing operators to control drones remotely from distant locations. Skladovs explained that from Riga, operators could control drones through the network far away, including over the Ukrainian frontlines.

The system leverages commercial mobile infrastructure rather than bespoke military radios. According to Salvis Skladovs, director of LMT Defence, drones using 4G or 5G can conceal their signals within the noise of ordinary civilian devices, making them harder to jam than dedicated military communications. This approach differs from satellite services like Starlink, which Russian forces have reportedly jammed and which faces service restrictions that have reportedly stopped Ukraine from intercepting Russian drones. Skladovs noted that Russia tries to avoid destroying mobile infrastructure because invading forces also depend on it.

LMT Defence has already supplied 5G drones to the Norwegian armed forces for use in tactical mobile networks. The company also functions as a systems integrator, helping militaries migrate command-and-control systems to 5G platforms. Through its research activities, LMT has developed its own 5G core network technology based on Open5GS, an open-source codebase, and has conducted private 5G tests with the armed forces of Latvia and Ukraine. The company has grown from eight employees in mid-2024 to between 80 and 90 today, including around 40 engineers.

For network practitioners, the development signals growing military demand for dual-use cellular technology. Former Nokia executive Tommi Uitto noted at MWC in early 2025 that complementing legacy military systems with 4G and 5G offers roughly ten times the performance for one tenth the cost. As NATO members increase defense spending, the ability to piggyback on existing civilian infrastructure or deploy private 5G networks provides a sovereign alternative to satellite dependence while offering operational flexibility across national borders.
