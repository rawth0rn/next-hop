AST SpaceMobile [announced plans](https://www.satellitetoday.com/launch/2026/11/06/ast-spacemobile-to-build-its-own-rocket-to-deal-with-launch-crunch/) to build its own rocket to deploy its direct-to-device constellation, entering into a more than $1.3 billion engine supply and licensing deal with Firefly Aerospace.

The company will develop the Blacklift rocket, a 20-ton vehicle designed to launch five BlueBird satellites per mission. AST SpaceMobile will purchase 27 Miranda engines and nine Vira vacuum-variant engines from Firefly for the first three rockets, plus acquire a perpetual license to manufacture the engines itself. The companies called the agreement non-competitive, with Firefly retaining full ownership of its engine intellectual property. The deal calls for approximately $400 million in cash up front and 10 to 14 million shares, followed by $250 million for engine delivery and $250 million over three years for engineering support services. Targeted launch date is late 2028.

The move addresses a deployment bottleneck that has slowed the constellation buildout. AST SpaceMobile expected to have 45 BlueBird satellites in orbit by the end of this year, but launch availability tightened after Blue Origin’s New Glenn vehicle placed one commercial satellite into the wrong orbit in April and subsequently went out of service. By building Blacklift, AST SpaceMobile gains dedicated launch capacity rather than competing for rideshare slots or waiting on external providers. Chief Executive Officer Abel Avellan stated the rocket provides regular unconstrained access to orbit and favorable owners' economics.

For network operators watching direct-to-device services, the rocket represents a hedge against launch market volatility. AST SpaceMobile stated it will continue using other launch providers, with Blacklift supplementing that strategy. The company held more than $3.7 billion in cash at the end of June, funding the vertical integration while maintaining partnerships. Regular access to orbit on their own schedule could improve constellation density timelines and reduce dependency on single-source launch failures.
