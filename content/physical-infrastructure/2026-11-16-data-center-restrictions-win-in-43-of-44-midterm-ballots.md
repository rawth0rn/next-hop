Voters in at least 43 of 44 jurisdictions across the United States approved bans or restrictions on data center development during the November 5 midterm elections, according to [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4246888/data-center-champions-lose-badly-as-ballot-initiatives-target-ai-infrastructure.html). The only exception was a small town in North Dakota that rejected a construction restriction. In Texas, Democratic candidate Gina Hinojosa won the governor's race by making the slowing of data center construction a centerpiece of her campaign, becoming the first Democrat to hold that office since 1994.

The ballot measures addressed three core concerns: electricity demand and utility bills, water consumption and environmental impact, and the balance of economic benefits against public costs. Ohio served as the primary battleground with 21 of the 50 tracked measures statewide. Voters there and elsewhere approved outright bans, power-consumption thresholds, temporary permitting moratoriums, mandatory public votes, and requirements for developers seeking tax incentives. In Joliet Township, Illinois, a restriction passed with 80% of the vote. Hubbard, Ohio saw more than three-quarters of voters oppose new facilities. California cities including Alhambra and Monterey Park approved prohibitions on data centers, as did Edgewater, Florida, which passed a charter amendment opposing new facilities and expansions.

The results introduce significant risk into an industry already facing grid interconnection delays and rising construction costs. Wall Street reacted immediately, with tech stocks wiping out more than $2 trillion in wealth on November 5. Established data centers and projects with existing approvals are not automatically subject to new restrictions, but projects still seeking sites, zoning, or financing face heightened uncertainty. Alex Cordovil, research director with Dell'Oro Group, noted that developers will likely engage in jurisdiction shopping, seeking authorities most welcoming to data centers. He also expects increased legal fights between federal, state, and local governments as policy pulls in different directions.
