The United States now hosts 15 of the world's 20 largest hyperscale datacenter locations, with Northern Virginia alone accounting for 12% of global capacity, according to [The Register Networks](https://www.theregister.com/off-prem/2026/08/20/us-claims-15-of-the-worlds-top-20-hyperscale-datacenter-locations/5290436).

Hyperscale facilities operate at massive scale, typically exceeding 100 megawatts of power capacity and spanning hundreds of thousands of square feet. Individual campuses may contain multiple buildings, each drawing more power than a small city. The concentration in Northern Virginia reflects the region's dense fiber infrastructure, proximity to Washington DC's federal data needs, and established power grids. These facilities house tens of thousands of servers and serve cloud providers, AI training clusters, and content delivery networks that require low latency access to the internet backbone. The scale creates distinct networking challenges, including the need for high-count fiber bundles, massive east-west traffic between racks, and redundant connections to multiple carriers. Cooling and power density requirements also drive network topology decisions, as liquid cooling systems and power distribution units compete for space with fiber trays and switch racks.

For network engineers and infrastructure architects, this geographic concentration creates both opportunities and constraints. The density in Northern Virginia means interconnection costs remain low within the region, but it also creates choke points for international traffic and increases vulnerability to regional power or cooling failures. A single fiber cut or electrical substation outage can impact multiple major providers simultaneously. Operators building AI training clusters or expanding cloud presence must negotiate for scarce power and real estate in these established hubs, or accept higher latency by locating in secondary markets. The dominance also shapes peering strategies, as major exchange points in Ashburn and surrounding areas become critical junctions for global traffic. Network planners must account for this concentration when designing redundancy and failover paths, particularly as AI workloads drive demand for even larger facilities.
