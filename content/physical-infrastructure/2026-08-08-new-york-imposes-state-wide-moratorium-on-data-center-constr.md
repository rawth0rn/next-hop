[New York imposed a state-wide moratorium on new data center construction](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4200222/data-center-energy-constraints-and-moratoriums-are-mounting-expect-to-see-stalled-ai-projects.html) in mid-July, halting permits for one year while the state studies energy impacts and requires developers to pay more for power or supply their own.

The order cites threats to utility bills and natural resources. It arrives as data center power demands shift from traditional 10 to 25 megawatt facilities to 100 megawatt expansions, with some builds reaching 2 gigawatts and one planned Louisiana project targeting 5 gigawatts. That 5 gigawatt figure equals the power needed for 4 million homes.

Research from North Carolina State University, Carnegie Mellon University, the University of Pittsburgh, and the University of Toronto projects data centers will increase average electricity costs by 6% to 29% by 2030, with some states seeing increases up to 57%. U.S. electricity consumption remained relatively level for three decades, leaving an aging grid now struggling to accommodate rapid load growth.

For network operators, the constraint is immediate. According to a May Flexential survey, power is now the main barrier to AI deployment nationwide, with 89% of technology decision makers citing reliable grid access as a critical factor and 72% expressing concern about electricity price volatility. Data center vacancy rates in North America sit at 0.9%, and most capacity through 2027 is already sold, leaving little room for expansion even where permitted.

Developers are responding with on-site generation. Oracle recently committed to 2.45 gigawatts of fuel cells for its New Mexico Project Jupiter facility, and a Bloom Energy survey found 61% of developers plan to deploy on-site power if grid connections fail. However, transmission limitations persist. A draft Department of Energy report released in early July identified transmission as a critical bottleneck, noting that even when generation exists, delivery infrastructure often does not.

The moratorium signals that energy planning is now inseparable from network capacity planning.
