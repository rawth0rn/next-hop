London still holds 70 percent of UK data center capacity, but regional markets are adding multi-megawatt projects as power constraints limit expansion in the capital, according to a report from [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/systems/2026/08/10/london-still-dominates-britains-datacenter-map-but-the-regions-are-powering-up/5284693).

The UK added 185 megawatts of new data center supply in 2024. London remains the dominant market with 70 percent of total capacity, yet regional cities including Manchester, Birmingham, and Edinburgh now attract multi-megawatt developments. Operators increasingly face power availability limits in London, while regional sites offer lower construction costs and accessible utility capacity.

This geographic shift directly impacts construction planning and network architecture. Regional data centers typically land further from core internet exchanges and fiber routes, adding latency for some workloads. However, available power and lower real estate costs offset that penalty for batch processing and storage-centric applications. Construction timelines also compress outside London where permitting and power grid connections move faster. These combined factors make regional builds attractive for certain workload types.

For practitioners planning capacity, the split demands a workload-specific strategy. Low-latency edge services still require London presence, but bulk compute and disaster recovery can sit in regional facilities at lower cost. Network architects must evaluate backhaul capacity from regional sites to ensure adequate connectivity. Power planners should note that regional utilities may offer more predictable expansion timelines than constrained London grids. This emerging dual-market approach requires careful mapping of application requirements to geographic capabilities.

The trend continues through 2025 as developers commit to regional projects. London's share will likely decline as a percentage, though absolute capacity there will still grow where power permits. Network operators should track both markets, matching workload requirements to the evolving geographic supply across the UK. This ongoing evolution creates a two-tier market with distinct cost and performance profiles that practitioners can exploit with proper planning.
