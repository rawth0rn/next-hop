A preprint on arXiv identifies specific technical and regulatory gaps that threaten the transition from 5G to 6G cellular networks. The paper, [Challenges and Opportunities in the Transition from 5G to 6G Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12147), uses case studies and statistical analysis to compare current 5G limitations against 6G requirements.

The research focuses on three operational pressure points: spectrum management, security architecture, and data transmission protocols. The authors employ a comparative methodology to assess how existing 5G infrastructure handles emerging bandwidth and latency demands, then map exactly where those systems fail to meet projected 6G specifications. They isolate specific vulnerabilities in spectrum allocation strategies and core network security that could delay deployment or expose critical infrastructure during the anticipated migration window.

The study also examines specific regulatory frameworks that lag behind technical capabilities. It notes that current policies may not accommodate the spectrum sharing and automated management techniques that 6G envisions. The statistical analysis quantifies performance gaps between legacy 5G deployments and the resilience targets set for 6G, providing operators with measurable benchmarks for infrastructure readiness.

For network operators, the paper offers a checklist of failure modes to audit before hardware refresh cycles begin. The authors propose concrete alternatives to current spectrum management practices and security frameworks, aiming to prevent the vulnerabilities from becoming permanent fixtures in early 6G rollouts. These recommendations include updated encryption standards and dynamic spectrum access protocols that could be trialed on existing 5G standalone cores. Operators should verify whether their current radio access networks can support the proposed dynamic allocation schemes without extensive hardware replacement. The work remains at the preprint stage and has not yet undergone peer review, so the findings should be treated as preliminary indicators rather than settled engineering guidance.
