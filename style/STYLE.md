# Next Hop Style Guide

The writer model follows this file exactly. Edit it any time; the next run
picks it up. The monthly calibration job may update the tunable sections
based on the owner's hand-edits to published posts, but the hard rules
section is preserved verbatim.

## Hard rules (enforced by QA, a post that breaks them is rejected)

- No em-dashes and no en-dashes. Use commas, periods, or parentheses. Write ranges as "800G to 1.6T".
- No arrows of any kind, neither unicode nor ascii.
- Never write "incl."; write "including".
- 250 to 500 words per post.
- Every factual claim traces to the cited source. Scope claims down rather than assert breadth the source does not support. If the source says "up to 30 percent", do not write "30 percent".

## Voice

- Plain prose a practitioner would write to another practitioner. No marketing register.
- Active voice. Short sentences. One idea per paragraph.
- Confident but literal: say what shipped, what changed, what it costs, what it replaces.
- Skepticism is allowed and welcome when the source itself hedges. Name the hedge.
- No hype vocabulary: banned words are game-changer, revolutionary, unleash, leverage (as a verb), delve, seamless, robust (as filler), cutting-edge, next-generation (as filler).

## Structure

- Open with the concrete thing that happened, in one sentence, with the source linked on first mention.
- Then one or two paragraphs of technical substance: what it is, how it works, what it connects to.
- Then why it matters for people who run networks: cost, scale, operations, interop, timelines.
- Close plain. No "exciting times ahead", no "only time will tell", no rhetorical questions.

## Vocabulary

- Expand acronyms on first use except truly universal ones (IP, TCP, DNS, GPU, AI, RF, 5G).
- Numbers with units, not adjectives: "51.2 Tb/s", not "blazing fast".
- Vendor names plain, no honorifics: "Broadcom shipped", not "industry leader Broadcom".

## Research posts

- Name the paper or draft, the venue, and the stage: preprint, working group draft, published RFC, peer-reviewed paper at a named conference.
- One sentence on method before results when the method is the point.
- Do not overstate: a measurement study "finds" or "measures", it does not "prove".
